Project summary

In short: We trained a Unitree Go2 to find a cube, move behind it, and push it into a goal. A high-level policy decides how the robot should move; a separate locomotion policy turns that command into stable joint motion. The real system connects both policies to camera-based tracking, state estimation, and ROS 2.

01

Why a hierarchy?

Pushing an object looks simple until locomotion and manipulation have to happen at the same time. The robot must keep its balance, maintain the cube in view, approach it from a useful direction, and still make progress toward a goal that is defined in the world frame.

We separated those responsibilities. The locomotion policy only has to walk reliably, while the pushing policy can work at a slower rate and reason about the robot, cube, and goal. This also gave us a practical route from simulation to the real Go2: the same low-level controller can be tested on its own before the complete perception stack is enabled.

03

Training in two stages

Both policies were trained with PPO in Isaac Lab, but not at the same time. We first trained a general velocity-tracking policy and then embedded that exported policy inside the pushing environment. During the second stage, the high-level policy learns to use the locomotion controller rather than learning individual joint motion from scratch.

The Go2 follows forward, lateral, and yaw commands over varied terrain. Rewards favor velocity tracking and stable, energy-conscious gait behavior while penalizing falls, sliding, joint-limit violations, and abrupt actions.

Curriculum

Command limits and reward emphasis are introduced gradually. Early training gives more help for approaching and aligning; later training puts more weight on moving the cube efficiently and completing the task.

Sim-to-real preparation

We randomize robot and cube mass, friction, motor gains, cube scale, spawn geometry, and floor properties. Cube observations also receive increasing delay, Gaussian noise, dropout, and occasional spikes.

04

What the policies see

The interface between the two policies is intentionally small. The high-level network produces a desired body velocity; the low-level network receives that command alongside fast proprioceptive feedback. The latest high-level command is held between its 10 Hz updates while locomotion continues at 50 Hz.

05

Tracking the cube

A single vision method was not reliable enough for the control loop. YOLOE gives a strong semantic detection but is comparatively slow. PCL can update more frequently, but it needs a good target association. We use the two together.

  1. 1

    8 Hz

    Segment

    A TensorRT YOLOE segmentation model searches for the open-vocabulary class box in a 640 × 360 image.

  2. 2

    RGB-D

    Lift to 3D

    Mask pixels are mapped back to aligned depth, sampled, deprojected, and filtered with the median absolute deviation.

  3. 3

    30 Hz

    Track in PCL

    The cloud is voxelized, the floor and front-leg points are removed, and plausible cube clusters are associated with the predicted state.

  4. 4

    EKF

    Fuse

    Timestamped YOLO and PCL positions update a planar position-velocity filter with friction-aware prediction and delayed-measurement replay.

Why replay delayed measurements?

Segmentation results arrive later than their camera exposure. The tracker keeps a short state history, inserts the measurement at its original timestamp, and runs the following updates again. This avoids treating an old observation as if it were current.

06

From simulation to the Go2

State estimation

Invariant EKF odometry

For robot pose and velocity, we adapted the INRIA Go2 odometry stack and its Invariant EKF. The filter propagates IMU data and corrects it with contact-aware leg kinematics. We moved the ROS wrapper to C++ so the 500 Hz path uses the native filter API directly.

Policy runtime

ROS 2 and TensorRT

The policies are exported to TensorRT engines and run in separate C++ nodes. The low-level node also owns the stand-up sequence, command timeout, torque limiting, CRC generation, and publication of Unitree motor commands.

Y

Set the goal

The current robot position in odometry becomes the center of the goal region. RViz updates the target marker immediately.

B

Recover the cube

If tracking is lost, commands ramp down. Recovery rotates toward the last known bearing until YOLO sees the cube near the front again, with one full turn as the search limit.

✓

Finish safely

The policy stops only after the cube remains inside the target for the hold time and the robot has moved clear of the goal area.

08

Current limitations

  • Cube tracking still depends on suitable depth coverage and a sufficiently visible object surface.
  • Odometry drift affects the shared world frame and therefore both goal placement and cube velocity.
  • The trained policy and perception thresholds are specific to the current Go2, camera placement, and cube geometry.
  • The recovery behavior is a deliberate search rotation, not a learned exploration strategy.
